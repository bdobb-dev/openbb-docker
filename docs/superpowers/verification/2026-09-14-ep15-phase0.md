## 13. Parallel walk + the manifest rewrite (`754eed3`, 2026-09-17)

Profiled first, on the calibration vault. The walk is **CPU-bound in our own code**, not on the
network — per symbol-week: parse + silver write + bars = 4.5 s (small), 17 s (median), ~8 min
(TSLA, 6.0M prints), against ~8–14 s of fetch. Threads would queue on the GIL, so the fan-out is
processes.

The larger avoidable cost was not the network either: `_default_manifest_row_writer` read and
OVERWROTE the whole 262,345-row manifest **per item** — 4–16 s each (calibration never hit it;
`calibrate` writes no manifest rows). A targeted `DeltaTable.update` of just the mutable columns
does the same job in 0.08–0.12 s and removes the documented lost-update hazard (`754eed3`).

Parallel design, keeping every invariant: workers fetch, parse and write their own append-only
bronze/silver rows and build the reconciliation REPORT; the parent stays the only writer of
`ops.backfill_manifest`, the only caller of `Gate`, and the only writer of
`ops.data_quality_issue`, ruling in dispatch order so gate sequence numbers are deterministic.
Concurrent appends measured safe: 6 processes × 10 appends × 5,000 real rows = 300,000 rows, no
failures, no lost commits. `vault backfill --loop --workers N` (default 1 = today's serial loop).

Measured end to end on two disjoint groups of six real symbol-weeks:

| | 6 items | per item | 262,345 items |
|---|---|---|---|
| serial | 164.7 s | 27.4 s | ~83 days |
| 6 workers | 61.8 s | 10.3 s | **~31 days** |

Same outcomes both ways (10 COMPLETE, 2 PARTIAL from reconciliation holds, 0 worker errors).
2.7×, not 6×: `pool.map` over a batch waits for its slowest item, so one heavy symbol idles the
other five workers.

### 13a. Streaming refill: measured +9%, and the benchmark that could not see it (`ff71d89`)

`run_fetch_stream` replaces the batch barrier — `workers` items stay in flight and the next one
dispatches the moment any worker frees. Gate sequence numbers are assigned **at dispatch**, so
ordering stays deterministic no matter what order results come back in, and the parent keeps every
manifest, gate and DQ write.

The first benchmark repeated the shape above — 6 items on 6 workers — and reported 11.4 s/item,
*slower* than batch. That number was meaningless: six items across six workers is a single wave
with nothing left to refill, so both designs reduce to "wait for the slowest item". A benchmark
can only see a refill if there are more items than workers. Rerun with 18:

| | items | per item | 262,345 items |
|---|---|---|---|
| serial | 6 | 26.2 s | ~80 days |
| batch, 6 workers | 6 | 10.3 s | ~31 days |
| **streaming, 6 workers** | **18** | **9.4 s** | **~28.5 days** |

So the barrier was worth about **9%**, not the large win the "obvious next step" framing implied —
2.8× on six cores. What is left is not the barrier: the parent-side manifest/gate/DQ writes
serialise every item, and per-item fetch time varies several-fold. Per-worker `SpanMemory` is still
unshared, so each worker re-learns a slow symbol's span.

Shipping this also exposed a real bug the tests could not: `LoopConfig` is frozen, so
`--workers N` raised `FrozenInstanceError` on the production path. Every loop test used a bare stub
context with no `.config`; `test_backfill_loop_workers_flag_replaces_the_frozen_config` now drives
a real `LoopContext`.
